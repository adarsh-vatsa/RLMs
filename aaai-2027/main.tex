\begin{abstract}
When an input is longer than a model's context window, an overflow policy decides what to remove, and long-context evaluations commonly cut out the middle of the prompt. At the same token budget, we compared middle truncation with query-aware truncation, which retrieves the chunks of the input that best match the question and keeps them in their original order. One open-weight model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled, served every policy on AA-LCR, MRCR v2 and LongBench-v2, at context windows of 32K to 262K tokens. Middle truncation cost much of the score: on AA-LCR at a 64K window, it scored 40\%, against 65\% with the whole input, and on MRCR at 262K it fell from 60\% to 10\% as conversations grew to 1.06M tokens. Query-aware truncation recovered most of this loss. A configuration of chunking, ranking and packing, specified before any reported run, scored 64\% on AA-LCR and 63\% on the longest MRCR conversation, significantly above middle truncation at every window on both benchmarks. On LongBench-v2, where the whole input gained only 5.9 points over middle truncation, its gains were not significant. Ranking the same chunks without the question gained less, although on AA-LCR keeping text from every document also helped. On MRCR, where the evidence could be located, the configuration's shortfall against an oracle came almost entirely from prompts that lacked part of the passage to be reproduced. Evaluations that truncate overflowing inputs therefore measure their overflow policy, in effect a retriever that ignores the question, as well as the model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When an input is longer than a model's context window, an \emph{overflow policy} decides which text to remove. Long-context evaluations commonly cut out the middle of the prompt \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2,zhang2024infinitebench,huang2026atlas}, which often drops the answer \cite{arjmandi2026distractor}, so their scores measure the overflow policy as well as the model. In retrieval terms, such a harness runs a retriever that ignores the question and does not report its evidence recall. Even modest overflow can be costly: in our experiments on AA-LCR at an 80K window, middle truncation keeps 74\% of each prompt on average, yet scores 20 points below full context. The choice is not confined to benchmarks: a retrieval-augmented system must fit its evidence to a token budget, and an agent harness must fit a growing history of turns and tool outputs into the window at every step \cite{packer2023memgpt}. Retrieval offers another policy: rank chunks of the documents against the question and keep the best that fit, in their original order. This is order-preserving retrieval as in OP-RAG and DOS RAG \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}, so we contribute a controlled evaluation, not a new method. We call it \emph{query-aware truncation}: it shortens the original prompt, as truncation does, but uses the question to choose what to keep.

Retrieval is usually compared with a full input that fits the window, rather than with truncation of one that does not. We compare overflow policies at the same token budget on three benchmarks whose answers depend on different things: reasoning across several documents (AA-LCR), reproducing one passage of a long conversation, a test of conversation memory (MRCR v2), and answering a question about one long text (LongBench-v2). One open-weight model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, serves every policy at windows of 32K to 262K tokens, and two policies at the same window differ only in the source text the model sees. The comparison has three parts: truncation; query-aware truncation, which ranks chunks by BM25, by dense embeddings or by both; and, on MRCR, an oracle that ranks the needed text first. Controls that rank chunks by position or at random, ignoring the question, show how much of the gain comes from choosing text by the question.

We test query-aware truncation mainly through one configuration, the \recipe{}, which fuses the BM25 and dense rankings and adds four steps to chunking and packing; we specified it before any run reported here. At the same budget, it recovers most of what middle truncation loses: on AA-LCR at a 64K window it scores 64\%, against 40\% for middle truncation and 65\% for full context, and on MRCR at 262K it matches the oracle. Its gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on these two benchmarks, but at none on LongBench-v2, where full context itself is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K. Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it, although on AA-LCR keeping text from every document also helps, and neither BM25 nor fusion ranking dominates. On MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where its prompt drops part of the passage to be reproduced, and at 64K on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 it cannot be told apart from full context while sending about half the input tokens.

Two lines of prior work come close. \citet{arjmandi2026distractor} showed the confound with closed models on inputs of up to about 180K tokens, against a truncation that removes only text a benchmark marks as irrelevant, which on MRCR resembles our oracle; we use an open-weight model, inputs of up to 1.06M tokens and a policy that needs no such labels. LongBench-v2 already sets order-preserving retrieval against its middle-truncated full input \cite{bai2025longbenchv2}, and OP-RAG and DOS RAG compare it with long-context reading and other retrieval pipelines \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}; we add controls that ignore the question at the same budget, an oracle, windows of 32K to 262K, and two more benchmarks, one over separate documents and one that asks for a passage reproduced exactly. On MRCR, we also split exact match into evidence recall at the token budget and exact match given the evidence. We will release our code, prompts and per-answer outputs.

\section{Related Work}
Retrieval helps models with short and long windows alike \cite{xu2024retrieval}; full context scores higher on average but costs more, which motivates routing each query to one or the other \cite{li2024selfroute}; and adding retrieved passages helps at first, then hurts \cite{jin2025longcontext}. Order-preserving retrieval, which keeps retrieved chunks in their source order, beats ordering them by score \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag} and can score above the full context with fewer tokens \cite{yu2024oprag,bai2025longbenchv2}. Choosing the parts of a document that match a query predates these systems: passage retrieval ranks documents by their best passages \cite{callan1994passage,kaszkiel1997passage}, and query-biased summaries show a reader the sentences that match the query \cite{tombros1998query}.

Middle truncation is the protocol of LongBench, LongBench-v2 and $\infty$Bench \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2,zhang2024infinitebench} and of a recent harness that includes AA-LCR, LongBench-v2 and another version of MRCR \cite{huang2026atlas}; HELMET instead cuts long documents from the end \cite{yen2025helmet}, and RULER generates each input at the length it tests \cite{hsieh2024ruler}. Middle truncation removes the part of a long input that models use least well \cite{liu2024lost}, yet it confounds studies of context length \cite{arjmandi2026distractor}. Our evidence-recall analysis applies the answer-in-context diagnostic of \citet{bala2026recall}, which asks whether the answer survives packing, to context windows of 32K to 262K tokens rather than budgets of a few hundred.

Prompt compression and context pruning drop tokens or sentences with a model that reads the question \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua,chirkova2025provence}, and other methods select context inside the model, from a memory of earlier blocks or from the key-value cache \cite{xiao2024infllm,li2024snapkv}. Agentic methods instead read the input over several model calls, recursing over the prompt \cite{zhang2025rlm} or searching it with file tools \cite{belcak2026longcontext}. We keep one model call and standard retrieval rankers.

\section{Benchmarks}
The served model reads up to 262K tokens, which holds every AA-LCR prompt and every LongBench-v2 prompt we keep. On these two benchmarks we therefore impose a 64K context window, so that every prompt overflows, and use the 262K run of the same prompt as the full-context reference. MRCR uses the model's full 262K window, which three of its four conversations exceed; only the shortest, which fits, has a full-context reference. We call these windows, 64K on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 and 262K on MRCR, each benchmark's \emph{default window}. Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} summarizes the benchmarks.

\paragraph{AA-LCR.} Artificial Analysis Long Context Reasoning \cite{artificialanalysis2025aalcr} asks questions over sets of 2 to 24 documents (median 6), mostly company documents. Answering can require combining facts across documents and computing a result, and an LLM judge grades the free-text answers (see Experiments).

\paragraph{MRCR v2.} Multi-round coreference resolution (MRCR) v2 \cite{vodrahalli2024michelangelo} hides four identical requests for a piece of writing, each answered differently, in a long synthetic conversation, and asks the model to reproduce one of the responses, the target, after a given random string. We use the four-needle release, its conversations of 133K, 267K, 533K and 1.06M tokens and 30 evenly spaced questions from each. It is the only benchmark here whose evidence can be located. An answer counts as correct only if it is the random string followed by the target response exactly; the benchmark's released score, which gives partial credit by string similarity, runs about 1 to 4 points higher for every policy in the main text and never reverses their order.

\paragraph{LongBench-v2.} LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2025longbenchv2} has 503 four-choice questions over long texts in six categories. We keep the 221 whose full prompt overflows the 64K window but fits the model's 262K, so that each has a full-context reference. The model answers with a single letter and no reasoning, so scoring is exact.

\section{Methodology}
A prompt holds fixed instructions, one or more source documents and a question. Its input budget is the context window minus the tokens reserved for the answer, counted on the fully rendered request with the served model's tokenizer and chat template. A prompt that fits is sent unchanged. For a longer one, every policy keeps the instructions and the question whole, and all policies share one pipeline that is otherwise the same. \emph{Middle truncation} cuts the last message of the rendered request by token count, keeping equal numbers of tokens from its start and end and silently dropping the middle; two variants, which mark the cut or keep only the start, are in the appendix. \emph{Selection} instead rebuilds the request from a subset of its source text (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Table~\ref{tab:policies} lists the policies; within each of its groups, the selection policies share chunking and packing and differ only in their ranking.

\paragraph{Chunking.} Each document is split into overlapping chunks, measured with the embedding model's tokenizer. Unless stated otherwise, chunks are 7,500 tokens with 750 of overlap, safely below our 8,192-token cap on embedding input, and 3,500 with 350 on MRCR.

\paragraph{Ranking.} A ranking orders all chunks of a prompt, with no top-$k$ cut. Three rankings use the question: BM25 ($k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$, on lower-cased words) \cite{robertson2009bm25}; dense, by cosine similarity between Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B embeddings of each chunk and of an instruction-prefixed query \cite{zhang2025qwen3embedding}; and fusion, by reciprocal-rank fusion ($k = 60$) of the two \cite{cormack2009rrf}. The query is the question on AA-LCR and the question with its four choices on LongBench-v2. On MRCR, a fixed pattern extracts from the question the description of the requested item (its format, topic and style), because the prefix the answer must start with is random and the ordinal (``the second poem'') never appears in the conversation. The needle requests repeat this description almost word for word, which favors lexical matching. Two controls ignore the question. \emph{Position} ranks each chunk by how many chunks separate it from the start or the end of its document, whichever is nearer, taking the documents in order on ties; it is middle truncation applied to each document rather than to the whole prompt. \emph{Random} shuffles the chunks with a fixed seed per question.

\paragraph{Packing and rendering.} Chunks are added in rank order for as long as the re-rendered prompt fits the budget. Text that is kept, with overlapping chunks merged, goes back into the original prompt template in its original order, with an omission marker wherever text was dropped inside a document. On AA-LCR, a document with nothing kept is left out. By default, packing stops at the first chunk that does not fit, so selection sends fewer tokens than truncation at the same budget: under 1\% fewer at MRCR's 262K window, 3--6\% at 64K and 7--15\% at 32K. \emph{Fill} packing, used by the \recipe, instead keeps adding lower-ranked chunks that still fit, then trims the best remaining chunk to the leftover budget.

\paragraph{The \recipe.} The configuration in our primary comparisons ranks by fusion and adds four steps to the default chunking and packing: (1) chunks of 1,024 tokens with 128 of overlap; (2) each ranked chunk widened by one neighboring chunk on each side; (3) fill packing; and (4) a document floor, which places the best chunk of every document before any document's second chunk. The floor answers a pilot on AA-LCR, in which dense retrieval left at least one document out for 63 of 100 questions. A plan written before any run reported here fixed these steps, a cumulative ablation that adds them to fusion one at a time on MRCR and LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, in the appendix), and the rule that its last configuration becomes the recipe; the appendix logs the order of all runs. The \emph{position recipe} and the \emph{BM25 recipe} take the same four steps but rank by position or by BM25 alone, so each differs from the \recipe{} only in its ranking.

\paragraph{Oracle.} On MRCR, the oracle offers first the chunks that overlap the target turn, then those that overlap the other needle turns, then the rest in the \recipe's order; chunk content, packing and prompt format are the recipe's. It shows what the recipe's prompt achieves when ranking never misses the answer.

\paragraph{Evidence recall.} Each answer records which source text its prompt kept; Figure~\ref{fig:spans} draws it for one AA-LCR question. On MRCR we can also tell whether each prompt holds the complete target turn and all needle turns; a turn missing any of its text is incomplete. Exact match then splits into a retrieval factor and a reading factor: \emph{evidence recall} at the token budget, the share of prompts that hold the complete target turn, and exact match on those prompts.

\section{Experiments}
\paragraph{Setup.} We serve Qwen3.6-35B-A3B \cite{qwen2026qwen36}, an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with a 262K-token context window, with vLLM 0.19.1 \cite{kwon2023vllm} on four NVIDIA L40S GPUs and send it one request at a time. Decoding is greedy with thinking disabled: a reasoning trace would take thousands of tokens from the same window and so shrink, and vary, the input budget the policies share. AA-LCR uses its official prompt and MRCR v2 its released prompts. On LongBench-v2 a system prompt asks for a single letter, and decoding is constrained to A--D. The answer reserve is 4,096 tokens on AA-LCR and MRCR and 8 on LongBench-v2. Unless stated otherwise, results are at each benchmark's default window (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}).

\paragraph{Conditions.} Most policies of Table~\ref{tab:policies} run at every window: 32K, 64K and 80K on AA-LCR, whose prompts all fit at 128K; 32K, 64K, 128K and 262K on MRCR; and 32K, 64K and 128K on LongBench-v2. Random chunks runs three seeds at each default window and full context once, at 262K. The oracle runs on MRCR only and the position recipe on AA-LCR only: on a prompt that holds one text, position is in effect middle truncation with an omission marker. Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, in the appendix, lists every condition.

\paragraph{Grading.} MRCR and LongBench-v2 are scored deterministically. AA-LCR is graded as its v1.1 release specifies \cite{artificialanalysis2025aalcr,artificialanalysis2026methodology}: an LLM judge, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna at medium reasoning effort, applies the published equality checker. To check Luna, we also graded every answer with the served model and labeled by hand every answer the two graders disagree on, plus a random sample of those they agree on. We estimate Luna's accuracy at 95.3\% (95\% interval [84.9, 99.1]), and the errors we found change no run's accuracy by more than two points (Table~\ref{tab:grader}, in the appendix). Answers that reach the 4,096-token cap, 4.6\% of AA-LCR answers, are mostly graded incorrect, but they do not explain the \recipe's lead over middle truncation (see the appendix).

\paragraph{Statistics.} Each condition runs once. We report paired differences from middle truncation at the same window, with 95\% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Questions that share a source are resampled together (the 30 document sets of AA-LCR and the 194 texts of LongBench-v2). MRCR has only four conversations, so we resample questions within each, and its intervals hold for these four conversations only. The plan posed two comparisons as the paper's central questions: the \recipe{} against middle truncation, and against position, which keeps the same structure without the question. We test both at every window on every benchmark, 20 comparisons in all, with a paired randomization test, correct the 20 $p$-values with Holm's method (Table~\ref{tab:primary}, in the appendix), and call a difference significant only when its corrected $p$-value is below 0.05. Every other comparison is exploratory, and we report its interval without correction. Our intervals do not cover the serving stack: a third node, whose runs we replaced under a rule set before rerunning, moved AA-LCR scores by up to 6 points (see the appendix).

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the policies at each benchmark's default window, and Figure~\ref{fig:policies} follows the main policies across windows; Table~\ref{tab:windows}, in the appendix, gives the numbers at every window. Differences are in points, against middle truncation at the same window unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Middle truncation costs much of the score, and query-aware truncation recovers most of it.} On AA-LCR at 64K, middle truncation scores 40\%, against 65\% for full context, and the \recipe{} scores 64\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}). On MRCR at the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the conversation that fits to 10\% on the longest, while the recipe scores the same as the oracle on every conversation, 63\% on the longest (Figure~\ref{fig:mrcr-length}, in the appendix). At this window the 133K conversation fits and 97\% of the 267K one does, so the gain comes from the two longest conversations, 60 of the 120 questions. MRCR's extracted query helps: querying with the full question instead costs dense ranking 9 points (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). LongBench-v2 leaves little to recover: full context is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K, a gain confined to three of its six categories (Table~\ref{tab:categories}, in the appendix). The recipe's gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on AA-LCR and MRCR, and at none on LongBench-v2 (Holm-corrected randomization tests, Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Changing the truncation itself does not help: marking the cut or keeping only the start of the documents moves the score by at most 2 points (Table~\ref{tab:other}, in the appendix).

\paragraph{Ranking by the question helps, and on AA-LCR so does keeping every document.} Random chunks and position, which ignore the question, never beat middle truncation with an uncorrected interval that excludes zero (Tables~\ref{tab:main}, \ref{tab:windows} and~\ref{tab:other}). At each benchmark's default window, every policy that ranks by the question does, except dense ranking on LongBench-v2 (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:other}). Since every policy at a window sends about the same number of tokens, what separates them is the text they keep, not the shorter context to which \citet{gao2026ink} trace most of the benefit of filtering. In the primary test, the \recipe{} is significantly better than position at every window on MRCR and at 32K and 64K on AA-LCR, though not at 64K with the third node's run, and not at 80K or on LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Position has none of the recipe's steps, so these margins mix ranking with the steps; on AA-LCR, the position recipe, which takes the steps but ranks by position, separates the two. The steps help even without the question: the position recipe keeps text from nearly every document and gains 21 points over middle truncation at 32K, while position leaves at least one document out for 86 of the 100 questions (Figure~\ref{fig:spans}). Fusion ranking adds 9 to 16 points to the same steps, with intervals that exclude zero at two of the three windows, but BM25 ranking adds 1 to 11, with an interval that excludes zero only at 80K. Neither ranking dominates: with the steps, BM25 leads fusion at 7 of the 10 windows, but only two of the differences have intervals that exclude zero, one in each direction: fusion leads on AA-LCR at 64K, by 11 points (5 with the third node's run), and BM25 on MRCR at 64K, by 12 (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The BM25 recipe needs no embedding model.

\paragraph{On MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where it misses the target.} In Table~\ref{tab:visibility}, no prompt with an incomplete target turn gets an exact match, as expected of a task that copies the target. Where the \recipe{} keeps the target complete, it is never more than one answer behind the oracle on the same questions. The oracle is not an upper bound, though: dropping the other needle turns changes the task (Table~\ref{tab:needles}, in the appendix). What the recipe misses is the target itself: at 32K its evidence recall is 48\% and it recovers 43\% of the oracle's gain over middle truncation, and both rise to 100\% at 262K. The BM25 recipe has higher evidence recall and so recovers more at every window below 262K. When the needles are guaranteed, less context helps, as input length alone degrades performance \cite{levy2024same,gao2026ink}: the oracle scores 75.0\% at 32K against 61.7\% at 262K (+13.3 [+4.2, +22.5], Table~\ref{tab:windows}).

\paragraph{On about half the input tokens, the recipe cannot be told apart from full context.} On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, where the model could read every prompt whole, a smaller window is also an input-token budget. At 64K, the \recipe{} differs from full context by $-$1.0 points [$-$8.7, +8.0] on AA-LCR and +0.5 [$-$4.0, +4.8] on LongBench-v2, while sending 58\% and 48\% of full context's input tokens in total (Tables~\ref{tab:windows} and~\ref{tab:cost}, in the appendix).


\section{Limitations}
All results come from one model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled; a reasoning phase might make up for missing text or compound its loss. On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 the window is imposed on a model that reads 262K, and a model built for a 64K window may use it differently. The samples are small (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}). Pilots shaped some choices: one on AA-LCR motivated the floor, and MRCR's release, query and default chunk size changed after an eight-needle pilot on which dense retrieval tied truncation. The position and BM25 recipes were designed, and the test for the primary comparisons chosen, after the \recipe's results were known. We did not test keeping only the end of the input (recency truncation), a cross-encoder reranker or prompt compression \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua}. Adding the recipe's steps one at a time cannot show which step helps most. On LongBench-v2, the three random seeds span 39.8 to 46.6\%, as wide as the recipe's gain over middle truncation; without a no-context run we cannot tell how much of its score the model's priors explain, and its questions leave out the prompts over 262K, which overflow without an imposed window. MRCR is synthetic, and its query nearly repeats the needle requests, which favors lexical ranking. The oracle and evidence recall need the evidence's location, so their account of why selection falls short rests on MRCR alone. AA-LCR scores depend on an LLM grader, whose accuracy has a 95\% lower bound of 84.9\% and which graded 3 of 276 recurring answers inconsistently, and on a 4,096-token answer cap, which answers reach more often under middle truncation than under the recipe, and never under full context.

\section{Conclusion}
In line with \citet{arjmandi2026distractor}, long-context evaluations that cut out the middle of overflowing prompts measure their overflow policy as well as the model. At the same token budget, query-aware truncation recovers most of what middle truncation loses on AA-LCR and MRCR, and ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it.

Evaluations that must truncate should therefore report their overflow policy, how much of each prompt it keeps and, where the evidence is marked, its evidence recall, alongside their scores. Future work should test other models, enable thinking, and extend the oracle and evidence recall to benchmarks of real documents that mark where the evidence is.

\paragraph{Serving nodes.} Every reported run was served by one of two nodes with the same GPUs.
