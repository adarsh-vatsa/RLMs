\begin{abstract}
\begin{notstarted}
When a long-context input is longer than the model's window, something must be removed. Benchmark harnesses usually cut out the middle of the prompt. We study this \emph{overflow policy} directly and treat retrieval as query-aware truncation: it re-renders the same prompt but keeps the chunks most similar to the question. On AA-LCR, MRCR v2 and LongBench-v2, served by one open-weight model at the same token budget, we compare it with naive truncation, structural and random controls, lexical retrieval and an oracle. The abstract will report the recipe's scores against middle truncation and full context, how much of the oracle's gain it recovers, and how few input tokens it needs to come within a point of full context.
\end{notstarted}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When an input is longer than a model's context window, an \emph{overflow policy} decides which text to remove. Long-context evaluations commonly cut out the middle of the prompt \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2,huang2026atlas}, which often drops the answer \cite{arjmandi2026distractor}. Even modest overflow can be costly: in our experiments on AA-LCR at an 80K window, middle truncation keeps 74\% of each prompt on average, yet scores 20 points below full context. Retrieval offers another policy: rank chunks of the documents against the question and keep the best that fit, in their original order. This is order-preserving retrieval as in OP-RAG and DOS RAG \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}, so we contribute a controlled evaluation, not a new method. We call it \emph{query-aware truncation}: it shortens the original prompt, as truncation does, but uses the question to choose what to keep.

Retrieval is usually compared with a full input that fits the window, rather than with truncation of one that does not. We compare overflow policies at the same token budget on three benchmarks whose answers depend on different things: reasoning across several documents (AA-LCR), reproducing one passage of a long conversation (MRCR v2), and answering a question about one long text (LongBench-v2). One open-weight model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, serves every policy at windows of 32K to 262K tokens, and two policies at the same window differ only in the source text the model sees. The comparison has three parts: truncation; query-aware truncation, which ranks chunks by BM25, by dense embeddings or by both; and, on MRCR, where the needed text can be located, an oracle that ranks that text first. Controls that rank chunks by position or at random, ignoring the question, show how much of the gain comes from choosing text by the question.

Our main method, the \recipe{}, combines the BM25 and dense rankings and adds four steps to chunking and packing; we fixed its settings on MRCR and LongBench-v2, holding out AA-LCR. It recovers most of what truncation loses. On AA-LCR at a 64K window, it scores 64\%, against 40\% for middle truncation and 65\% for full context. On MRCR at the model's 262K window, it matches the oracle on every conversation, scoring 63\% on the longest, of 1.06M tokens, against 10\% for middle truncation. Its gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on these two benchmarks, but at none on LongBench-v2, where full context itself is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K. Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it. At smaller windows on MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where its prompt drops part of the passage to be reproduced. With the same steps, BM25 ranking alone, which needs no embedding model, does about as well. Where the model could read the whole prompt, a smaller window also saves input tokens: the recipe comes within a point of full context while sending 58\% as many on AA-LCR and about a quarter as many on LongBench-v2.

Our contributions are this controlled comparison, per-answer provenance of what the model saw (Figure~\ref{fig:spans}), and a visibility decomposition on MRCR, which splits exact match into the share of prompts that hold the whole passage to be reproduced and exact match on those prompts.

\section{Related Work}
Comparisons of retrieval with long-context models usually set a retrieved context against the full one. Retrieval helps models with short and long windows alike \cite{xu2024retrieval}; full context scores higher on average but costs more, which motivates routing each query to one or the other \cite{li2024selfroute}; and adding retrieved passages helps at first, then hurts \cite{jin2025longcontext}. Order-preserving retrieval, which keeps retrieved chunks in their source order, beats ordering them by score \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag} and can score above the full context with fewer tokens \cite{yu2024oprag,bai2025longbenchv2}. We use order-preserving retrieval as an overflow policy and compare it with truncation at the same token budget, on inputs longer than the context window.

Middle truncation is the protocol of LongBench and LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2} and of a recent harness that includes all three of our benchmarks \cite{huang2026atlas}. \citet{arjmandi2026distractor} shows that it confounds studies of context length: it often drops the answer, while removing only the text that a benchmark's construction marks as irrelevant, in effect an oracle, largely preserves accuracy. That study uses closed models and inputs of up to about 180K tokens. On MRCR, we measure how much of an oracle's gain ranking by the question alone recovers, with one open-weight model and conversations of up to 1.06M tokens. Our visibility analysis applies the answer-in-context diagnostic of \citet{bala2026recall}, which asks whether the answer survives packing, to context windows of 32K to 262K tokens rather than budgets of a few hundred.

Prompt compression and context pruning drop tokens or sentences with a model that reads the question \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua,chirkova2025provence}. Agentic methods instead read the input over several model calls, recursing over the prompt \cite{zhang2025rlm} or searching it with file tools \cite{belcak2026longcontext}. We keep one model call and standard retrieval rankers, so that the policies differ only in which source text the model sees.

\section{Benchmarks}
We use three benchmarks, which differ in what an answer depends on: reasoning across several documents (AA-LCR), reproducing one passage of a long conversation (MRCR v2), and answering a four-option question about one long text (LongBench-v2). The served model reads up to 262K tokens, which holds every AA-LCR prompt and every LongBench-v2 prompt we keep. On these two benchmarks we therefore impose a 64K context window, so that every prompt overflows, and use the 262K run of the same prompt as the full-context reference. MRCR needs no imposed window: it uses the model's full 262K context window, which three of its four conversations exceed, and only the shortest, which fits, has a full-context reference. Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} summarizes them.

\paragraph{AA-LCR.} Artificial Analysis Long Context Reasoning \cite{artificialanalysis2025aalcr} asks questions over sets of documents, mostly company documents (63 questions), followed by government consultations, industry reports, legal, marketing, academic and survey documents. A question comes with 2 to 24 documents (median 6). Answering can require finding the relevant documents, combining facts across them and computing a result. Answers are free text, and an LLM judge decides whether each matches the reference (see Experiments).

\paragraph{MRCR v2.} Multi-round coreference resolution (MRCR) v2 \cite{vodrahalli2024michelangelo} presents a long synthetic conversation in which the user repeatedly asks for a piece of writing with a given format, topic and style, such as ``write a poem about penguins in archaic style''. The same request is made four times, and each time the assistant writes a different response. We call these four turns, each a request and its response, the \emph{needles}. The final question asks the model to repeat one of them, for example the second, which we call the \emph{target}, and to start its answer with a given random string. The needles can be found in the conversation, so this is the only benchmark here on which we know where the answer is. We use the four-needle release and take its conversations of 133K, 267K, 533K and 1.06M tokens and 30 evenly spaced questions from each. An answer counts as correct only if it is the random string followed by the target response exactly; the benchmark's released scorer instead gives partial credit by string similarity.

\paragraph{LongBench-v2.} LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2025longbenchv2} has 503 four-choice questions over long texts. We keep the 221 whose full prompt overflows the 64K context window but fits the model's 262K; a longer prompt would have no full-context reference. They cover single-document QA (82 questions), multi-document QA (46), long in-context learning (44), dialogue history (20), structured data (17) and code repositories (12). The model answers with a single letter and no reasoning, so scoring is exact.

\section{Methodology}
A prompt holds fixed instructions, one or more source documents and a question. Its input budget is the context window minus the tokens reserved for the answer, counted on the fully rendered request with the served model's tokenizer and chat template. A prompt that fits is sent unchanged and an \emph{overflow policy} decides which source text to keep for a longer one. Every overflow policy keeps the instructions and the question whole, and all of them run in one pipeline that is otherwise the same, so two conditions on a benchmark differ only in the source text the model sees.

\subsection{Truncation Policies}
Truncation cuts the last message of the rendered request by token count. \emph{Middle truncation}, the protocol of LongBench and LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2}, keeps equal numbers of tokens from the start and the end of that message and silently drops the middle. \emph{Marked middle truncation} makes the same cut but inserts an omission marker, which tests whether knowing that text is missing helps the model. \emph{Head} keeps only the start of the documents, followed by the whole question.

\subsection{Selection Policies}
Selection rebuilds the request from a subset of its source text in three stages: chunking, ranking, and packing and rendering. Unless stated otherwise, every selection policy uses the same chunking and packing, so two of them differ only in their ranking.

\paragraph{Chunking.} Each document is split into overlapping chunks, measured with the embedding model's tokenizer. Unless stated otherwise, chunks are 7,500 tokens with 750 of overlap, and 3,500 with 350 on MRCR.

\paragraph{Ranking.} A ranking orders all chunks of a prompt, with no top-$k$ cut. Three rankings use the question: BM25 ($k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$, on lower-cased words) \cite{robertson2009bm25}; dense, by cosine similarity between Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B embeddings of each chunk and of an instruction-prefixed query \cite{zhang2025qwen3embedding}; and fusion, by reciprocal-rank fusion ($k = 60$) of the two \cite{cormack2009rrf}. The query is the question on AA-LCR and the question with its four choices on LongBench-v2. On MRCR, it is the description of the requested item (its format, topic and style), because the prefix the answer must start with is random and the ordinal (``the second poem'') never appears in the conversation. The needle requests repeat this description almost word for word, which favors lexical matching. Two controls ignore the question. \emph{Position} ranks each chunk by how many chunks separate it from the start or the end of its document, whichever is nearer, taking the documents in order on ties. It is middle truncation applied to each document rather than to the whole prompt. \emph{Random} shuffles the chunks with a fixed seed per question. Because both controls share the chunks, packing and rendering of the three question-aware rankings, comparing them separates the effect of the prompt's structure from that of choosing text by the question.

\paragraph{Packing and rendering.} Chunks are added in rank order for as long as the re-rendered prompt fits the budget. Text that is kept, with overlapping chunks merged, goes back into the original prompt template in its original order, as in order-preserving retrieval \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}, with an omission marker wherever text was dropped inside a document. On AA-LCR, a document with nothing kept is left out. By default, \emph{stop} packing stops at the first chunk that does not fit, so selection sends fewer tokens than truncation at the same budget: under 1\% fewer at MRCR's 262K window, 3--6\% at 64K and 7--15\% at 32K. \emph{Fill} packing, used by the \recipe, instead keeps adding lower-ranked chunks that still fit, then trims the best remaining chunk to the leftover budget.

\paragraph{The \recipe.} Our main method ranks by fusion and adds four steps to the default chunking and packing: (1) chunks of 1,024 tokens with 128 of overlap; (2) each ranked chunk widened by one neighbouring chunk on each side; (3) fill packing; and (4) a document floor, which places the best chunk of every document before any document's second chunk. We added the steps one at a time, as the rungs of an ablation ladder on MRCR and LongBench-v2 (see Experiments). The \emph{position recipe} and the \emph{BM25 recipe} take the same four steps but rank by position or by BM25 alone, so each differs from the \recipe{} only in its ranking.

\paragraph{Oracle.} Because the needles can be found in the conversation, we can build an oracle from the \recipe. Chunks that overlap the target turn are offered first, then those overlapping the other needle turns, then the rest in the recipe's order. The information on the needle's location is only used to rank the chunks; chunk content, packing and prompt format are the recipe's. The oracle shows what the recipe's prompt achieves when ranking never misses the answer.

\paragraph{Provenance and visibility.} Each answer records the character ranges its prompt kept, and truncated prompts can be rebuilt exactly; Figure~\ref{fig:spans} draws these ranges for one AA-LCR prompt. On MRCR, we can tell whether each prompt holds the complete target turn and all needle turns; we count a turn as incomplete if any of its text is missing from the prompt. Exact match then splits into two factors (Table~\ref{tab:visibility}): the share of prompts that hold the complete target turn, and exact match on those prompts.

\section{Experiments}
\paragraph{Setup.} We serve Qwen3.6-35B-A3B \cite{qwen2026qwen36}, an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with a 262K-token context window, with vLLM 0.19.1 \cite{kwon2023vllm} on four NVIDIA L40S GPUs and send it one request at a time. Decoding is greedy with thinking disabled. AA-LCR uses its official prompt and MRCR v2 its released prompts. On LongBench-v2 a system prompt asks for a single letter, and decoding is constrained to A--D. The answer reserve is 4,096 tokens on AA-LCR and MRCR and 8 on LongBench-v2. Within a benchmark, every condition uses the same questions, prompt template, model and serving setup, and conditions are compared question by question. Unless stated otherwise, results are at each benchmark's context window in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}: 64K on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, and the model's full 262K on MRCR.

\paragraph{Conditions.} A condition is one policy at one context window on one benchmark, with each random seed counted separately. Table~\ref{tab:conditions} lists all 93, grouped by what they test. Every window keeps the same answer reserve. AA-LCR stops at 80K because every prompt fits 128K, while at 80K none fits and middle truncation keeps 74\%. Only MRCR has an oracle, because only its evidence can be located. It also has no full-context condition: three of its four conversations exceed 262K, and on the shortest, middle truncation at 262K already sends every prompt whole. We fixed the \recipe{} on MRCR and LongBench-v2 at their context windows: an ablation ladder added its four steps to fusion one run at a time, and the final rung became the recipe before any run of it on AA-LCR or at another window. AA-LCR, the only benchmark whose questions come with several separate documents, is therefore held out: no setting of the recipe was chosen on its scores. The document floor has no effect on a single document, so it could not be tested on MRCR or LongBench-v2. On AA-LCR, after the recipe was fixed, we ran it without the floor at every window, and the ladder's first two rungs at 64K, which complete the ladder there. Two dense variants on MRCR give it the two settings that the other benchmarks use by default: one queries with the full question, the other uses 7,500-token chunks. The position and BM25 recipes, which serve as controls, were designed after the \recipe's results were known. MRCR and LongBench-v2 have no position recipe: each of their prompts holds one text, on which position keeps the head and the tail, in effect marked middle truncation. Window sweeps cover only the policies that Figure~\ref{fig:policies} follows and, on AA-LCR, the recipe without the floor; full context runs at 262K, and every other condition at the benchmark's context window.

\paragraph{Grading.} MRCR and LongBench-v2 are scored deterministically against their reference answers. AA-LCR is graded by the benchmark's equality checker, an LLM judge (GPT-5.6 Luna at medium reasoning effort), with the official grader prompt and the benchmark's rules for equivalent answers added \cite{artificialanalysis2026methodology}. To check Luna, we also graded every answer with the served model. We then labeled by hand every answer the two graders disagree on, plus a random sample of those they agree on, without seeing either grade. We estimate Luna's accuracy at 95.3\% (95\% interval [84.9, 99.1]), and its errors change no run's accuracy by more than two points (Table~\ref{tab:grader}, in the appendix). AA-LCR's prompts do not ask for an answer format, and with thinking disabled the model often works through the problem in its answer; the judge grades the value the answer finally commits to. Across all AA-LCR runs, 4.6\% of answers reach the 4,096-token cap, and Luna marks 136 of these 144 as incorrect.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Each condition runs once. We report paired differences from middle truncation at the same window, with 95\% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples drawn with a fixed seed. Questions that share a source are resampled together (the 30 document sets of AA-LCR and the 194 texts of LongBench-v2). MRCR has only four conversations, so we resample questions within each conversation, and its intervals hold for these four conversations only. Two comparisons, which we chose after the results were known, answer the paper's central questions: the \recipe{} against middle truncation, and against position, which keeps the same structure without taking the question into account. We test both at every window on every benchmark, 20 comparisons in all, with a two-sided paired randomization test that swaps the two policies' outcomes in the same groups as the bootstrap (100,000 swaps), and correct the 20 $p$-values together with Holm's method (Table~\ref{tab:primary}, in the appendix). We call a difference significant only when its corrected $p$-value is below 0.05. Every other comparison is exploratory, and we report its interval without correction. Greedy decoding is repeatable in our setup: the two serving nodes that ran every reported run gave identical answers to identical prompts (see the appendix).
\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the policies at each benchmark's context window, and Figure~\ref{fig:policies} follows the main policies across windows; Table~\ref{tab:windows}, in the appendix, gives the numbers at every window. Differences are in points, against middle truncation at the same window unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{The \recipe{} recovers most of what truncation loses.} On AA-LCR at 64K, the \recipe{} scores 64\%, against 40\% for middle truncation and 65\% for full context; at 32K, from about a quarter of each prompt, it still scores 58\%, against 21\% for middle truncation (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:windows}). LongBench-v2 leaves little to recover, since full context is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K. There, at 64K, every policy that ranks by the question scores within a point of full context or above it (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and at 128K, where half the prompts fit and every policy sends them whole, none gains more than 3 points over middle truncation (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). On MRCR, at the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the conversation that fits to 10\% on the longest, while the recipe scores the same as the oracle on every conversation, 63\% on the longest (Figure~\ref{fig:mrcr-length}, in the appendix). Changing the truncation itself does not help: marking the cut or keeping only the head moves the score by at most 2 points (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The recipe's gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on AA-LCR and MRCR, and at none on LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).

\paragraph{Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it.} Position and random chunks rank the same chunks as BM25, dense and fusion, but without taking the question into account, and they never beat middle truncation with an interval that excludes zero (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:windows}). Every policy that ranks by the question scores above middle truncation at each benchmark's context window, with an uncorrected interval that excludes zero for all but dense on LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). In the primary test, the \recipe{} is significantly better than position on MRCR at every window, by 23 to 30 points, and on AA-LCR at 32K and 64K, by 31 and 18 points, but not at 80K or on LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Position has none of the recipe's steps, so these margins mix ranking with the steps. On AA-LCR, the position recipe, which takes the steps but ranks by position, separates the two. The steps help even without the question: the position recipe gains 21 points over middle truncation at 32K and keeps text from nearly every document, while position leaves at least one out for 86 of the 100 questions. Fusion ranking still adds 9 to 16 points to the steps, with intervals that exclude zero at 32K and 64K, and BM25 ranking adds 1 to 11, with an interval that excludes zero only at 80K (Table~\ref{tab:windows}).

\paragraph{The steps help, and no single ranking wins everywhere.} Given to BM25, the \recipe's four steps add 10 to 21 points on MRCR below 262K and 12 to 13 on AA-LCR at 32K and 80K, and change little elsewhere (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). Added one at a time on the ladders, only one step has an interval that excludes zero: the neighbouring chunks, on AA-LCR (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, in the appendix). On AA-LCR, where the ladder ran after the recipe was fixed, the rung that adds this step scores 67, above the recipe's 64; we did not change the recipe. The document floor, which only AA-LCR can test, changes the recipe by at most 2 points (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). MRCR's own query and chunk size matter: querying with the full question, or using the 7,500-token chunks of the other benchmarks, costs dense about 10 points there (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). With the default chunks, BM25, dense and fusion cannot be told apart (Table~\ref{tab:main}). With the recipe's steps, the BM25 recipe leads the \recipe{} at 7 of the 10 windows (Table~\ref{tab:windows}), but only one lead each way has an interval that excludes zero: fusion's on AA-LCR at 64K, and BM25's on MRCR at 64K, as expected of a query that nearly repeats the needle requests. We keep the \recipe{} as our main method because it was fixed before the controls were designed, with AA-LCR held out; switching now would leave no benchmark held out. The BM25 recipe is the alternative that needs no embedding model.

\paragraph{On MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where it misses the target.} In Table~\ref{tab:visibility}, no prompt with an incomplete target turn gets an exact match. Where the \recipe{} keeps the target complete, it is never more than one answer behind the oracle on the same questions. What it misses is the target itself: at 32K it keeps the target complete for 48\% of questions and recovers 43\% of the oracle's gain over middle truncation, and both rise to 100\% at 262K. The BM25 recipe keeps the target complete more often and so recovers more at every window below 262K. When the needles are guaranteed, less context helps: the oracle scores 75.0\% at 32K against 61.7\% at 262K (+13.3 [+4.2, +22.5]).

\paragraph{The \recipe{} comes within a point of full context on fewer tokens.} On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, where the model could read every prompt whole, a smaller window is an input-token budget. On LongBench-v2, the 32K window holds about a quarter of full context's 137K input tokens, yet the \recipe{} scores within a point of full context ($-$0.5 [$-$5.4, +4.7]) and the BM25 recipe 1.4 points above it, while middle truncation loses 7.7 (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The \recipe's gain over middle truncation there is not significant (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). On AA-LCR, the \recipe{} scores within a point of full context at 64K ($-$1.0 [$-$8.7, +8.0]), on 58\% of its input tokens, and 7 points below it at 32K. At 64K its answers are also about as long as answers to the full prompt, while answers to middle-truncated prompts are 55\% longer (Table~\ref{tab:cost}, in the appendix).

\section{Limitations}
All results come from one model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled. The samples are small (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}), and MRCR's intervals hold for its four conversations only. The recipe was fixed on the MRCR and LongBench-v2 questions we report, and MRCR's query and chunk size on MRCR itself; only AA-LCR is held out. The position and BM25 recipes were designed after the \recipe's results were known, and the position recipe ran on AA-LCR only. The ladders cannot show which of the recipe's steps helps most: only the neighbouring chunks on AA-LCR have an interval that excludes zero, and the same step lowers the score on LongBench-v2. MRCR is synthetic, and its query nearly repeats the needle requests, which favors lexical ranking. The oracle and the visibility decomposition need the evidence's location, known only on MRCR, so their account of why selection falls short rests on one synthetic benchmark. AA-LCR scores depend on an LLM grader, whose accuracy we can bound only at 84.9\% or more, and on a 4,096-token answer cap, which answers reach more often under middle truncation than under the recipe, and never under full context.

\section{Conclusion}
We compared overflow policies at the same token budget, treating retrieval as query-aware truncation. The \recipe{} scores significantly above middle truncation at every window on AA-LCR and MRCR v2, and at each benchmark's context window it recovers most of what truncation loses. The BM25 recipe, which needs no embedding model, does about as well. On LongBench-v2, where full context itself gains little over middle truncation, the recipe's gain is significant at no window. Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it. At smaller windows on MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where its prompt drops part of the target.

In line with \citet{arjmandi2026distractor}, evaluations that cut out the middle of overflowing prompts measure their overflow policy as well as the model: on MRCR at the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the conversation that fits to 10\% on the longest, where the recipe still scores 63\%. Query-aware truncation can also save input tokens where the model could read the whole prompt: the recipe comes within a point of full context while sending 58\% as many on AA-LCR and about a quarter as many on LongBench-v2. Future work should test other models, enable thinking, and extend the oracle and the visibility decomposition to benchmarks of real documents that mark where the evidence is.

\section{Appendix: Serving Nodes}
Every reported run was served by one of two nodes with the same GPUs. A third node, which first served the \recipe's runs and the BM25 runs at the windows other than each benchmark's context window, answers identical prompts differently from the other two. We reran those runs on one of the two nodes, as decided before the reruns and whatever their results, and report only the reruns. On the two nodes, greedy decoding is repeatable: the \recipe{} at each benchmark's context window builds the same prompts as the final ladder rung and repeats its answers to all 120 MRCR questions on the other node and to all 221 LongBench-v2 questions thirteen days later. On AA-LCR, the recipe with and without the document floor give the same answer to all 191 questions for which they built the same prompt.
