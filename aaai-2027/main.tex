\begin{abstract}
\begin{notstarted}
When a long-context input is longer than the model's window, something must be removed. Benchmark harnesses usually cut out the middle of the prompt. We study this \emph{overflow policy} directly and treat retrieval as query-aware truncation: it re-renders the same prompt but keeps the chunks most similar to the question. On AA-LCR, MRCR v2 and LongBench-v2, served by one open-weight model at the same token budget, we compare it with naive truncation, structural and random controls, lexical retrieval and an oracle. The abstract will report how much of the accuracy lost to truncation it recovers, and how much of the oracle's gain.
\end{notstarted}
\end{abstract}

\paragraph{AA-LCR.} Artificial Analysis Long Context Reasoning \review{cite AA-LCR} asks questions over sets of documents, mostly company documents (63 questions), followed by government consultations, industry reports, legal, marketing, academic and survey documents. A question comes with 2 to 24 documents (median 6). Answering can require finding the relevant documents, combining facts across them and computing a result. Answers are free text, and an LLM judge decides whether each matches the reference (see Experiments). \hl{Every prompt overflows the 64K context window.} 

\paragraph{MRCR v2.} Multi-round coreference resolution (MRCR) v2 \review{cite MRCR v2} presents a long synthetic conversation in which the user repeatedly asks for a piece of writing with a given format, topic and style, such as ``write a poem about penguins in archaic style''. The same request is made various times, and each time the assistant writes a different response. Model responses are called \emph{needles} and the final question asks the model to repeat one of them, which is the \emph{target}, by starting its answer with a random string. We use the four-needle release and take its conversations of 133K, 267K, 533K and 1.06M tokens and 30 evenly spaced questions from each. An answer counts as correct only if it is the random string followed by the target response exactly; the benchmark's released scorer instead gives partial credit by string similarity. \hl{Context window is the served model's full 262K. The 133K conversation fits and is sent whole, which makes it the only full-context reference.}

\paragraph{LongBench-v2.} LongBench-v2 \review{cite LongBench-v2} has 503 four-choice questions over long texts. We keep the 221 whose full prompt exceeds the 64K budget \hl{but fits the 262K window, so that every question has a full-context reference.} They cover single-document QA (82 questions), multi-document QA (46), long in-context learning (44), dialogue history (20), structured data (17) and code repositories (12). The model answers with a single letter and no reasoning, so scoring is exact.

\section{Methodology}
A prompt holds fixed instructions, one or more source documents and a question. Its input budget is the context window minus the tokens reserved for the answer. We count tokens on the fully rendered request, with the served model's tokenizer and chat template. A prompt that fits is sent unchanged and an \emph{overflow policy} decides what a longer prompt becomes. All \hl{benchmarks (replaced "policies")} run in one pipeline that is otherwise identical across conditions, and in every condition \hl{we report the instructions and the questions that are sent to the model}. Policies only differ depending on the source text they get.

\subsection{Truncation Policies}
Truncation cuts the rendered request by token count. \emph{Middle truncation}, \hl{the LongBench protocol} \review{cite LongBench}, keeps equal numbers of tokens from its start and end and silently drops the middle. \emph{Marked middle truncation} makes the same cut but inserts an omission marker, which tests whether knowing that text is missing helps the model. \emph{Head} keeps only the start of the context followed by the question.

\subsection{Selection Policies}
Selection rebuilds the same request from a subset of its source text in \hl{three steps: Chunking, Ranking and Packing.} Two selection policies differ only in their ranking. A selection policy is only applied if the source(s) overflow the context window.

\paragraph{Chunking.} Each document is split into overlapping chunks, measured with the embedding model's tokenizer. Unless stated otherwise, chunks are 7,500 tokens with 750 overlap. \hl{On MRCR, earlier runs use 3,500 tokens with 350 overlap, where with 7,500-token chunks, dense exact match on MRCR falls from 55.0\% to 45.0\% ($-$10.0 points [$-$17.5, $-$2.5]).}

\paragraph{Ranking.} A ranking orders all chunks of a prompt, with no top-$k$ cut. Three rankings use the question: BM25 ($k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$, on lower-cased words) \review{cite BM25}; dense, by cosine similarity between Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B embeddings of each chunk and of an instruction-prefixed query \review{cite Qwen3-Embedding}; and fusion, by reciprocal-rank fusion ($k = 60$) of the two \review{cite RRF}. The query is the question on AA-LCR and the question with its four choices on LongBench-v2. On MRCR it is the description of the requested item (its format, topic and style), because the prefix the answer must start with is random and the ordinal (``the second poem'') never appears in the conversation. The needle requests repeat this description almost word for word, which favours lexical matching. Two controls ignore the question. \emph{Position} orders chunks by their distance from the nearer end of their document, taking the documents in order at each distance, so heads and tails are kept first. \emph{Random} shuffles the chunks with a fixed seed per question. Position builds a prompt with the same structure as a retrieval prompt (documents in order, under their headers, with omission markers) without using the question, from the same chunks and with the same packing as BM25 and dense, so comparing it with them separates the effect of the prompt's structure from that of selection.

\paragraph{Packing and rendering.} Chunks are added in rank order for as long as the re-rendered prompt fits $B$. Kept spans are merged where they overlap or touch, put back in source order and rendered into the original prompt template, with an omission marker wherever text was dropped, as in order-preserving retrieval \review{cite OP-RAG, DOS RAG}. On AA-LCR, documents keep their original numbers, and a document with no kept span is left out. By default packing stops at the first chunk that does not fit, so selection sends fewer tokens than truncation at the same budget: under 1\% fewer at MRCR's 262K window, 3--6\% at 64K and 7--15\% at 32K. \emph{Fill} packing instead trims the best remaining chunk to the leftover budget.

\paragraph{The \recipe.} Our main method adds four steps to fusion, each a rung of an ablation ladder on MRCR and LongBench-v2 (see Experiments): 1,024-token chunks with 128 of overlap; each chunk widened by one neighbouring chunk on each side; fill packing; and a document floor, which offers the best chunk of every document before any document's second chunk.

\paragraph{Oracle.} MRCR marks which turns hold the answer, so we can build an oracle from the \recipe: chunks that overlap the target turn are offered first, then those overlapping the other needle turns, then the rest in the recipe's order. Only this order uses gold data; chunking, packing and rendering are unchanged. The oracle therefore shows what the same prompt format achieves when ranking never misses the answer.

\paragraph{Provenance and visibility.} Each answer records the character ranges its prompt kept, and truncated prompts can be rebuilt exactly. On MRCR we can therefore tell whether a prompt held the whole target turn and all needle turns, and split exact match into how often the target was visible and how often the model answered correctly when it was (Table~\ref{tab:visibility}). AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 do not release evidence locations, so this split is limited to MRCR.

\section{Experiments}
\paragraph{Setup.} We serve Qwen3.6-35B-A3B \review{cite Qwen3.6}, an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with a 262K-token window, with vLLM 0.19.1 \review{cite vLLM} on four NVIDIA L40S GPUs, and send it one request at a time. Decoding is greedy, with thinking disabled. AA-LCR uses its official prompt and MRCR v2 its released prompts. On LongBench-v2 a system prompt asks for a single letter, and decoding is constrained to A--D. The answer reserve is 4,096 tokens on AA-LCR and MRCR and 8 on LongBench-v2. Within a benchmark, every condition uses the same questions, prompt template, model and serving setup, and conditions are compared question by question. \review{Only one model family was run. Say so here, or only in Limitations?}

\paragraph{Conditions.} We ran the conditions in four stages. The first stage separates a prompt's structure from selection: marked middle truncation, head, position and random chunks (three seeds on AA-LCR, one elsewhere), next to middle truncation and dense retrieval, all at the main window. The second adds BM25 and fusion on all three benchmarks, and an ablation ladder on MRCR and LongBench-v2 only, which adds the \recipe's four steps to fusion one run at a time. No step's interval excludes zero on either benchmark. The last two rungs score the same, because the document floor has no effect on a single document. As planned, the final rung became the \recipe{}, fixed before any run of it on AA-LCR or at another window. \review{Per-step ladder numbers: an appendix table, or one sentence in Results?} On MRCR, a further dense run with the full question as its query scores 45.8\% against 55.0\% ($-$9.2 points [$-$15.0, $-$4.2]), which supports the request-description query. In the third stage, AA-LCR, the only multi-document benchmark, serves as the held-out check: no setting of the recipe was chosen on its scores, and the recipe runs on it once per window. Because the ladders could not test the document floor, we later also ran the recipe without it on AA-LCR. It scored 56\%, 63\% and 62\% at 32K, 64K and 80K against the recipe's 58\%, 64\% and 62\%. No difference is clear (the largest is +2.0 [$-$3.7, +8.9] at 32K), so the recipe stayed as fixed. The fourth stage adds two controls, designed after the recipe's results were known. Each takes the recipe's four steps and changes only the ranking: the \emph{position recipe} ranks by position, and the \emph{BM25 recipe} by BM25 alone. Both run on AA-LCR at 32K, 64K and 80K, and the BM25 recipe also on MRCR at 32K, 64K and 128K. MRCR has no position recipe, because on a single conversation position keeps the head and the tail, which is in effect marked middle truncation.

\paragraph{Budgets.} The main window is 64K on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, where every prompt overflows, and the model's full 262K on MRCR. Middle truncation, position, BM25 and the \recipe{} also run at 32K and 80K on AA-LCR, 32K, 64K and 128K on MRCR, and 32K and 128K on LongBench-v2, with the same answer reserve; on MRCR the oracle runs at every window. AA-LCR stops at 80K because every prompt fits 128K, while at 80K none fits and middle truncation keeps 74\%. Full context is the 262K run on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, in which every prompt fits.

\paragraph{Grading.} MRCR and LongBench-v2 are scored deterministically. AA-LCR's prompt asks for no answer format, and with thinking disabled the model often explains or works through the problem in its answer; 4.9\% of answers reach the 4,096-token cap, and nearly all of these are graded incorrect. Full context never reaches the cap; middle truncation does on 5 to 8 of its 100 answers at each window, and the recipe on 1 to 6. We grade with the benchmark's own equality checker, GPT-5.6 Luna at medium reasoning effort with the official grader prompt. \review{Cite the AA-LCR methodology page for the checker model; the repository records no source for it.} It sees the question, the reference and the answer, and judges the value the answer finally commits to. To check it, the served model also graded every answer. Over the 29 AA-LCR runs the two graders disagree on 40 of 2,900 answers. We label every disagreement by hand, mixed with randomly drawn agreements and without seeing either grade. \review{Labelling is incomplete after the rerun: the 19 disagreements and 16 sampled agreements of the 11 new runs wait in \texttt{grader-check-rerun.csv}. On the 18 earlier runs, Luna is right on 15 of the 21 disagreements and both graders are right on all 27 sampled agreements; Luna's six errors, four lenient and two strict, fall in five runs and change no run's accuracy by more than one point. Once labelled, give Luna's record on all 40, its estimated accuracy with an interval, and the largest change to a run's accuracy.} Luna's grades are also not fully repeatable: of the 263 answers that recur character for character in more than one run, it graded 3 differently from one run to another, while the served model graded every one the same.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Each condition runs once. We report paired differences from middle truncation at the same window, with 95\% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Questions that share a source are resampled together: the 30 document sets of AA-LCR and the 194 texts of LongBench-v2. MRCR has only four conversations, so we resample questions within each conversation, and its intervals hold for these four conversations only. Every comparison within a benchmark uses the same resamples. We call a difference clear when its interval excludes zero; the intervals are not corrected for the number of comparisons. For a reference that beats middle truncation, either full context or the MRCR oracle, we also report the share of its gain a policy recovers, $(s - s_{\mathrm{middle}}) / (s_{\mathrm{ref}} - s_{\mathrm{middle}})$, with its interval taken from the same resamples. Greedy decoding is repeatable in this setup. Two nodes with the same GPUs served every reported run, and identical prompts gave identical answers on both. On MRCR, 14 runs on one node and 2 on the other agree character for character on the 30 answers to the conversation that fits. The \recipe{} at the main window repeats the prompts of the final ladder rung and gives the same answer to all 120 MRCR questions, from the other node, and to all 221 LongBench-v2 questions, thirteen days later. On AA-LCR, the recipe and the recipe without the document floor give the same answer to all 191 questions for which they built the same prompt. Runs first served by a third node, which answered identical prompts differently, were rerun on one of these two and are not reported.

\paragraph{Cost.} Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports input tokens and time per question. Selection embeds documents with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B on one more L40S GPU. Each document set or conversation is embedded once, and the time is spread over the questions that share it. Selection time covers ranking and packing. Generation time is the time from sending the request to receiving the answer.

\paragraph{Structure alone recovers little, with one exception.} A selected prompt differs from a truncated one in two ways: it keeps spans of the documents in order, under their headers and with omission markers, and it chooses those spans by the question. Position builds the same structure without the question, from the same chunks and with the same packing as BM25 and dense. Its gain over middle truncation is at most 6 points and never clear at any window: +6.0 [$-$4.6, +16.5] on AA-LCR, +0.0 [$-$2.5, +2.5] on MRCR and +1.8 [$-$1.8, +5.6] on LongBench-v2 at the main windows. Random chunks are no better than middle truncation, and neither marking the cut nor keeping the head changes the score. The exception is the position recipe on AA-LCR at 32K, which gains +21.0 [+8.4, +32.7] over middle truncation and +15.0 [+4.1, +25.7] over position, still without the question. Its small chunks, fill packing and document floor change what a question-blind prompt holds: at 32K it leaves out a document for 6 of the 100 questions, where position does for 86. At 64K and 80K its gains over middle truncation, +12.0 [0.0, +22.7] and +8.0 [$-$5.2, +19.2], are not clear. On AA-LCR, then, part of what the \recipe{} recovers at a small window does not depend on the question: the position recipe recovers 48\% [23, 69] of the full-context gap at 32K.

\paragraph{Ranking by the question adds to structure.} Every question-aware ranking beats middle truncation at the main window, except dense on LongBench-v2 (+5.0 [$-$1.6, +11.7]). Against position itself, BM25 and dense gain +21.7 [+13.3, +30.0] and +16.7 [+9.2, +24.2] on MRCR. On each of the other two benchmarks one of the two is clear: dense on AA-LCR (+10.0 [+2.0, +19.3]; BM25 +8.0 [$-$2.4, +18.4]) and BM25 on LongBench-v2 (+6.3 [+1.3, +11.6]; dense +3.2 [$-$3.0, +9.5]). None of the three covers every document: on AA-LCR at 64K, position leaves out at least one document for 62 of the 100 questions, BM25 for 64 and dense for 63. The matched comparison on AA-LCR is the \recipe{} against the position recipe, which differ only in ranking. The \recipe{} gains +16.0 [+7.2, +24.2] at 32K and +12.0 [+2.9, +23.0] at 64K; at 80K its +9.0 [$-$0.8, +20.8] is not clear. The BM25 recipe separates from the position recipe only at 80K (+11.0 [+3.4, +20.0]; +7.0 [$-$6.2, +21.1] at 32K and +1.0 [$-$7.3, +10.7] at 64K). On MRCR, where position stays within 2 points of middle truncation, the \recipe{} gains between +23.3 and +30.0 over position at every window, every interval above zero. On LongBench-v2 it is not clearly better than position at any window (+5.0 [$-$2.3, +12.3], +4.5 [$-$0.9, +10.0] and +1.4 [$-$0.9, +3.8] at 32K, 64K and 128K).

\paragraph{The \recipe{} recovers most of what truncation loses on AA-LCR.} It recovers 84\% [65, 105], 96\% [68, 141] and 85\% [53, 125] of the accuracy middle truncation loses against full context at 32K, 64K and 80K. At 32K it scores 58\% from 27\% of each prompt, against 21\% for middle truncation and 65\% for full context, and at 64K its 64\% is level with full context ($-$1.0 [$-$8.7, +8.0]). LongBench-v2 leaves little to recover, since full context is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K. There, BM25 and the recipe score 48.9\% and 47.1\%, no worse than full context (46.6\%) on less than half its input tokens.

\paragraph{The steps help at small windows, and no ranking wins everywhere.} With the default chunks at the main window, dense ranking cannot be told apart from BM25 on any benchmark: BM25's difference from dense is $-$2.0 [$-$13.8, +7.9] on AA-LCR, +5.0 [$-$3.3, +13.3] on MRCR and +3.2 [$-$1.9, +8.3] on LongBench-v2, and fusing the two adds nothing over dense. The recipe's four steps add to fusion on AA-LCR (+11.0 [+1.8, +21.1]) but not clearly on MRCR (+5.8 [$-$0.8, +12.5]) or LongBench-v2 ($-$1.4 [$-$5.1, +2.3]). The BM25 recipe shows what the steps add when the ranking is fixed and the window is small. Over BM25 it gains +20.8 [+11.7, +30.0], +18.3 [+8.3, +28.3] and +10.0 [+1.7, +18.3] on MRCR at 32K, 64K and 128K, and +13.0 [+1.1, +26.6] and +12.0 [+2.9, +20.9] on AA-LCR at 32K and 80K, though nothing at 64K ($-$1.0 [$-$10.3, +10.9]). Part of this is packing: at 32K, BM25's larger chunks leave 15\% of the budget unused on AA-LCR and 9\% on MRCR, and fill packing leaves almost none. With the steps equal, the better ranking depends on the benchmark. On AA-LCR the \recipe{} leads the BM25 recipe by +11.0 [+5.0, +17.8] at 64K; its lead at 32K is not clear (+9.0 [$-$3.2, +21.2]), and at 80K it has none ($-$2.0 [$-$6.7, +3.5]). On MRCR the BM25 recipe leads: by 11.7 [+2.5, +20.8] at 64K, and not clearly by 9.2 [$-$1.7, +20.0] at 32K and 1.7 [$-$5.8, +9.2] at 128K. MRCR's query nearly repeats the needle requests, which favours lexical ranking, so this lead may not carry over to other tasks. LongBench-v2 has no such control; there BM25 stays within 2 points of the \recipe{} at every window, never clearly apart. The \recipe{} remains our main method because it was fixed before these runs, with AA-LCR held out; the BM25 recipe is the alternative that needs no embedding model. AA-LCR has no ladder and the controls take all four steps at once, so we cannot say which step makes the difference.

\paragraph{Across windows.} In Figure~\ref{fig:policies}, position tracks middle truncation, and the \recipe{} lies above both at every window on AA-LCR and MRCR. On LongBench-v2 every policy stays within 10 points at every window. There, the recipe's gain over middle truncation is +7.2 [+0.5, +14.2] at 32K and +6.3 [+0.9, +12.0] at 64K, but only +1.8 [$-$0.5, +4.5] at 128K, where middle truncation keeps 89\% of a prompt on average: 115 of the 221 prompts fit and every policy sends them whole, and on the other 106 the recipe gains 6 answers and loses 2.

\paragraph{Longer conversations.} At the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the 133K conversation, which it sends whole, to 27\% and 10\% on the 533K and 1.06M ones, of which 48\% and 24\% fit (Figure~\ref{fig:mrcr-length}). BM25 scores 60\% on every conversation, and the recipe matches the oracle on all four, at 70\% and 63\% on the two longest.

\paragraph{Ranking, not reading, limits the recipe on MRCR.} With the target turn hidden, exact match is 0\% in every row of Table~\ref{tab:visibility}. A policy's exact match is therefore the share of its prompts that show the target, times its exact match on those. Where the recipe shows the target, it answers as often as the oracle does on the same questions: 44 against 39 of 57 at 32K, 52 against 53 of 85 at 64K, and 67 against 63 of 107 at 128K. \review{These matched counts come from a one-off script over the predictions and needle spans; add them to \texttt{mrcr\_v2.visibility} so they are reproducible.} EM $\mid$ target is not comparable across rows, because a prompt that shows the target but hides other needles leaves the ordinal fewer candidates: middle truncation scores 82--86\% on the few targets it shows at 32K and 64K, where it almost never shows all four needles. What the recipe misses is the target itself, which it shows for 48\%, 71\%, 89\% and 100\% of questions from 32K to 262K. It therefore matches the oracle at 262K and at 128K, where the oracle leads by 3.3 [$-$3.3, +10.0]. It trails by 20.0 [+11.7, +28.3] at 64K and by 38.3 [+27.5, +49.2] at 32K. From 262K down to 32K, it recovers 100\%, 90\%, 59\% and 43\% of the oracle's gain over middle truncation. A better ranking closes more of the gap: the BM25 recipe shows the target for 65\%, 86\% and 98\% of questions at 32K, 64K and 128K, and recovers 57\% [43, 71], 83\% [67, 100] and 95\% [75, 118] of the oracle's gain. When the needles are guaranteed, less context helps: the oracle scores 75.0\% at 32K against 61.7\% at 262K (+13.3 [+4.2, +22.5]). On the 133K conversation, the only one with a full-context run, the oracle at 32K scores 70.0\% against full context's 60.0\% (+10.0 [$-$10.0, +30.0]). These 30 questions are too few to show that a focused prompt beats full context.

\paragraph{Cost.} On a GPU, selection costs little next to generation (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). BM25 adds 0.2\,s per question on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 and 1.0\,s on MRCR. Counting embedding, the recipe adds 1.4\,s, 3.6\,s and 3.7\,s. The BM25 recipe embeds nothing, and its selection takes 0.8\,s per AA-LCR question at 64K. On LongBench-v2, whose one-letter answers take 3.5\,s to generate, that roughly doubles the time per question. Full context sends 1.7 to 2.2 times as many input tokens as the 64K policies and is slower on both benchmarks that have a full-context run (9.7\,s against 8.2\,s on AA-LCR, 8.6\,s against 3.5\,s on LongBench-v2). On CPU, embedding took 91\,s per AA-LCR question and 22\,s per MRCR question, more than generation, which BM25 avoids entirely.

\section{Limitations}
All results come from one model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled, and from one run per condition; another model family or a reasoning mode may order the policies differently. The samples are small: 100 AA-LCR questions over 30 document sets, 120 MRCR questions over four conversations, whose intervals do not extend to other conversations, and 221 LongBench-v2 questions on which full context is only 5.9 points above middle truncation. We report many intervals without correcting for their number, and several that we call clear have a lower bound within a point of zero.

The recipe's four steps are a bundle we cannot take apart: no step is clear on the ladders, and the controls change its ranking, not its steps. The controls were designed after the recipe's results were known, ran on AA-LCR and MRCR only, and do not agree on a ranking: fusion leads on AA-LCR and BM25 on MRCR, each clearly at one window. Chunk size and the MRCR query were chosen on MRCR itself, and the ladders ran on the MRCR and LongBench-v2 questions we report; only AA-LCR is held out. MRCR is synthetic, and its query nearly repeats the needle requests, which favours lexical ranking. It is also the only benchmark on which we can measure visibility, because the other two do not release evidence locations. On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 the overflow is imposed: the model's window holds every prompt, and we set a smaller budget. Only MRCR overflows the served window, and only its shortest conversation has a full-context reference. AA-LCR scores depend on an LLM grader, whose accuracy our check bounds only at 88\% or more \review{update once the new runs are labelled} and whose grades vary slightly between calls, and on a 4,096-token answer cap that answers to truncated prompts reach more often than answers to full ones. Our LongBench-v2 protocol (a subset, our own system prompt and decoding constrained to one letter) is not the official one, so its scores are not comparable with published ones. Finally, the selection method is order-preserving retrieval and is not new; our contribution is the controlled comparison.
