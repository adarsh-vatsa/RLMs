\begin{abstract}
When an input is longer than a model's context window, an overflow policy decides what to remove, and long-context evaluations commonly cut out the middle of the prompt. At the same token budget, we compared middle truncation with query-aware truncation, which retrieves the chunks of the input that best match the question and keeps them in their original order. One open-weight model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled, served every policy on AA-LCR, MRCR v2 and LongBench-v2, at context windows of 32K to 262K tokens. Middle truncation cost much of the score: on AA-LCR at a 64K window, it scored 40\%, against 65\% with the whole input, and on MRCR at 262K it fell from 60\% to 10\% as conversations grew to 1.06M tokens. Query-aware truncation recovered most of this loss. A configuration of chunking, ranking and packing fixed on MRCR and LongBench-v2 scored 64\% on held-out AA-LCR and 63\% on the longest MRCR conversation, significantly above middle truncation at every window on both benchmarks. On LongBench-v2, where the whole input gained only 5.9 points over middle truncation, its gains were not significant. Ranking the same chunks without the question gained less, although on multi-document inputs keeping text from every document also helped. On MRCR, where the evidence could be located, the configuration's shortfall against an oracle came almost entirely from evidence that did not fit the budget. Evaluations that truncate overflowing inputs therefore measure their overflow policy, in effect a retriever that ignores the question, as well as the model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When an input is longer than a model's context window, an \emph{overflow policy} decides which text to remove. Long-context evaluations commonly cut out the middle of the prompt \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2,zhang2024infinitebench,huang2026atlas}, which often drops the answer \cite{arjmandi2026distractor}, so their scores measure the overflow policy as well as the model. In retrieval terms, such a harness runs a retriever that ignores the question and does not report its evidence recall. Even modest overflow can be costly: in our experiments on AA-LCR at an 80K window, middle truncation keeps 74\% of each prompt on average, yet scores 20 points below full context. The choice is not confined to benchmarks: a retrieval-augmented system must fit its evidence to a token budget, and an agent harness must fit a growing history of turns and tool outputs into the window at every step \cite{packer2023memgpt}. Retrieval offers another policy: rank chunks of the documents against the question and keep the best that fit, in their original order. This is order-preserving retrieval as in OP-RAG and DOS RAG \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}, so we contribute a controlled evaluation, not a new method. We call it \emph{query-aware truncation}: it shortens the original prompt, as truncation does, but uses the question to choose what to keep.

Retrieval is usually compared with a full input that fits the window, rather than with truncation of one that does not. We compare overflow policies at the same token budget on three benchmarks whose answers depend on different things: reasoning across several documents (AA-LCR), reproducing one passage of a long conversation, a test of conversation memory (MRCR v2), and answering a question about one long text (LongBench-v2). One open-weight model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, serves every policy at windows of 32K to 262K tokens, and two policies at the same window differ only in the source text the model sees. The comparison has three parts: truncation; query-aware truncation, which ranks chunks by BM25, by dense embeddings or by both; and, on MRCR, where the needed text can be located, an oracle that ranks that text first. Controls that rank chunks by position or at random, ignoring the question, show how much of the gain comes from choosing text by the question.

The cost of middle truncation grows with the overflow: on MRCR at the model's 262K window, it scores 60\% on the conversation that fits and 10\% on the longest, of 1.06M tokens. At the same budget, query-aware truncation recovers most of what middle truncation loses. We test it mainly through one configuration, the \recipe{}, which combines the BM25 and dense rankings and adds four steps to chunking and packing; we fixed it on MRCR and LongBench-v2, holding out AA-LCR. On AA-LCR at a 64K window, it scores 64\%, against 40\% for middle truncation and 65\% for full context, and on MRCR at 262K it matches the oracle on every conversation, scoring 63\% on the longest, with a query that nearly repeats the needle requests. Its gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on these two benchmarks, but at none on LongBench-v2, where full context itself is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K.

Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it, although on AA-LCR the recipe's steps recover part of the gain without the question. Neither ranking dominates: with the same steps, BM25, which needs no embedding model, leads fusion at some windows and trails it at others. At smaller windows on MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where its prompt drops part of the passage to be reproduced. Where the model could read the whole prompt, a smaller window also saves input tokens: at 64K, the recipe's score cannot be told apart from full context's on AA-LCR ($-$1.0 points [$-$8.7, +8.0]) or LongBench-v2 (+0.5 [$-$4.0, +4.8]), on 58\% and 48\% of full context's input tokens.

Our contribution is this controlled evaluation, and two lines of prior work come close. \citet{arjmandi2026distractor} showed the confound with closed models on inputs of up to about 180K tokens, against a truncation that removes only text a benchmark marks as irrelevant; we use an open-weight model, inputs of up to 1.06M tokens and a policy that needs no such labels. LongBench-v2 already sets order-preserving retrieval against its middle-truncated full input \cite{bai2025longbenchv2}, and OP-RAG and DOS RAG compare it with long-context reading and other retrieval pipelines \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}; we add controls that ignore the question at the same budget, an oracle where the evidence can be located, windows of 32K to 262K, and two more benchmarks, one over separate documents and one that asks for a passage reproduced exactly. Per-answer provenance of what the model saw (Figure~\ref{fig:spans}) and, on MRCR, a split of exact match into evidence recall at the token budget and exact match given the evidence serve as analysis tools. We will release our code, prompts and per-answer outputs.

\section{Related Work}
Comparisons of retrieval with long-context models usually set a retrieved context against the full one. Retrieval helps models with short and long windows alike \cite{xu2024retrieval}; full context scores higher on average but costs more, which motivates routing each query to one or the other \cite{li2024selfroute}; and adding retrieved passages helps at first, then hurts \cite{jin2025longcontext}. Order-preserving retrieval, which keeps retrieved chunks in their source order, beats ordering them by score \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag} and can score above the full context with fewer tokens \cite{yu2024oprag,bai2025longbenchv2}. Choosing the parts of a document that match a query predates these systems: passage retrieval ranks documents by their best passages \cite{callan1994passage,kaszkiel1997passage}, and query-biased summaries show a reader the sentences that match the query \cite{tombros1998query}.

Middle truncation is the protocol of LongBench, LongBench-v2 and $\infty$Bench \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2,zhang2024infinitebench} and of a recent harness that includes all three of our benchmarks \cite{huang2026atlas}; HELMET instead cuts long documents from the end \cite{yen2025helmet}, and RULER generates each input at the length it tests \cite{hsieh2024ruler}. Middle truncation removes the part of a long input that models use least well \cite{liu2024lost}, yet \citet{arjmandi2026distractor} shows that it confounds studies of context length: it often drops the answer, while removing only the text that a benchmark's construction marks as irrelevant, in effect an oracle, largely preserves accuracy. Our evidence-recall analysis applies the answer-in-context diagnostic of \citet{bala2026recall}, which asks whether the answer survives packing, to context windows of 32K to 262K tokens rather than budgets of a few hundred.

Prompt compression and context pruning drop tokens or sentences with a model that reads the question \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua,chirkova2025provence}, and other methods select context inside the model, from a memory of earlier blocks or from the key-value cache \cite{xiao2024infllm,li2024snapkv}. Agentic methods instead read the input over several model calls, recursing over the prompt \cite{zhang2025rlm} or searching it with file tools \cite{belcak2026longcontext}. We keep one model call and standard retrieval rankers, so that the policies differ only in which source text the model sees.

\section{Benchmarks}
We use three benchmarks, which differ in what an answer depends on: reasoning across several documents (AA-LCR), reproducing one passage of a long conversation (MRCR v2), and answering a four-option question about one long text (LongBench-v2). The served model reads up to 262K tokens, which holds every AA-LCR prompt and every LongBench-v2 prompt we keep. On these two benchmarks we therefore impose a 64K context window, so that every prompt overflows, and use the 262K run of the same prompt as the full-context reference. MRCR needs no imposed window: it uses the model's full 262K context window, which three of its four conversations exceed, and only the shortest, which fits, has a full-context reference. We call these windows, 64K on AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 and 262K on MRCR, each benchmark's \emph{default window}. Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} summarizes the benchmarks.

\paragraph{AA-LCR.} Artificial Analysis Long Context Reasoning \cite{artificialanalysis2025aalcr} asks questions over sets of documents, mostly company documents (63 questions), followed by government consultations, industry reports, legal, marketing, academic and survey documents. A question comes with 2 to 24 documents (median 6). Answering can require finding the relevant documents, combining facts across them and computing a result. Answers are free text, and an LLM judge decides whether each matches the reference (see Experiments).

\paragraph{MRCR v2.} Multi-round coreference resolution (MRCR) v2 \cite{vodrahalli2024michelangelo} presents a long synthetic conversation in which the user repeatedly asks for a piece of writing with a given format, topic and style, such as ``write a poem about penguins in archaic style''. The same request is made four times, and each time the assistant writes a different response. We call these four turns, each a request and its response, the \emph{needles}. The final question asks the model to repeat one of them, for example the second, which we call the \emph{target}, and to start its answer with a given random string. The needles can be found in the conversation, so this is the only benchmark here on which we know where the answer is. We use the four-needle release and take its conversations of 133K, 267K, 533K and 1.06M tokens and 30 evenly spaced questions from each. An answer counts as correct only if it is the random string followed by the target response exactly; the benchmark's released scorer instead gives partial credit by string similarity. Its score runs about 1 to 4 points above exact match for every policy in the main text and never reverses their order (Table~\ref{tab:mrcr-score}, in the appendix).

\paragraph{LongBench-v2.} LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2025longbenchv2} has 503 four-choice questions over long texts. We keep the 221 whose full prompt overflows the 64K context window but fits the model's 262K; a longer prompt would have no full-context reference. They cover single-document QA (82 questions), multi-document QA (46), long in-context learning (44), dialogue history (20), structured data (17) and code repositories (12). The model answers with a single letter and no reasoning, so scoring is exact.

\section{Methodology}
A prompt holds fixed instructions, one or more source documents and a question. Its input budget is the context window minus the tokens reserved for the answer, counted on the fully rendered request with the served model's tokenizer and chat template. A prompt that fits is sent unchanged and an \emph{overflow policy} decides which source text to keep for a longer one. Every overflow policy keeps the instructions and the question whole, and all of them run in one pipeline that is otherwise the same, so two conditions on a benchmark differ only in the source text the model sees.

\subsection{Truncation Policies}
Truncation cuts the last message of the rendered request by token count. \emph{Middle truncation}, the protocol of LongBench and LongBench-v2 \cite{bai2024longbench,bai2025longbenchv2}, keeps equal numbers of tokens from the start and the end of that message and silently drops the middle. Two variants, which mark the cut or keep only the start, are in the appendix.

\subsection{Selection Policies}
Selection rebuilds the request from a subset of its source text in three stages: chunking, ranking, and packing and rendering (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Table~\ref{tab:policies} lists the policies; within each of its groups, the selection policies share chunking and packing and differ only in their ranking.

\paragraph{Chunking.} Each document is split into overlapping chunks, measured with the embedding model's tokenizer. Unless stated otherwise, chunks are 7,500 tokens with 750 of overlap, safely below the embedding model's 8,192-token input limit, and 3,500 with 350 on MRCR.

\paragraph{Ranking.} A ranking orders all chunks of a prompt, with no top-$k$ cut. Three rankings use the question: BM25 ($k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$, on lower-cased words) \cite{robertson2009bm25}; dense, by cosine similarity between Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B embeddings of each chunk and of an instruction-prefixed query \cite{zhang2025qwen3embedding}; and fusion, by reciprocal-rank fusion ($k = 60$) of the two \cite{cormack2009rrf}. The query is the question on AA-LCR and the question with its four choices on LongBench-v2. On MRCR, it is the description of the requested item (its format, topic and style), because the prefix the answer must start with is random and the ordinal (``the second poem'') never appears in the conversation. The needle requests repeat this description almost word for word, which favors lexical matching. Two controls ignore the question. \emph{Position} ranks each chunk by how many chunks separate it from the start or the end of its document, whichever is nearer, taking the documents in order on ties. It is middle truncation applied to each document rather than to the whole prompt. \emph{Random} shuffles the chunks with a fixed seed per question. Because both controls share the chunks, packing and rendering of the question-aware rankings, comparing them separates the effect of the prompt's structure from that of choosing text by the question.

\paragraph{Packing and rendering.} Chunks are added in rank order for as long as the re-rendered prompt fits the budget. Text that is kept, with overlapping chunks merged, goes back into the original prompt template in its original order, as in order-preserving retrieval \cite{yu2024oprag,laitenberger2025dosrag}, with an omission marker wherever text was dropped inside a document. On AA-LCR, a document with nothing kept is left out. By default, packing stops at the first chunk that does not fit, so selection sends fewer tokens than truncation at the same budget: under 1\% fewer at MRCR's 262K window, 3--6\% at 64K and 7--15\% at 32K. \emph{Fill} packing, used by the \recipe, instead keeps adding lower-ranked chunks that still fit, then trims the best remaining chunk to the leftover budget.

\paragraph{The \recipe.} The configuration in our primary comparisons ranks by fusion and adds four steps to the default chunking and packing: (1) chunks of 1,024 tokens with 128 of overlap; (2) each ranked chunk widened by one neighboring chunk on each side; (3) fill packing; and (4) a document floor, which places the best chunk of every document before any document's second chunk. The floor answers a pilot on AA-LCR, in which dense retrieval left at least one document out for 63 of 100 questions. A plan written before any run reported here fixed these steps, a ladder that adds them to fusion one at a time on MRCR and LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, in the appendix), and the rule that its last rung becomes the recipe. The recipe ran on AA-LCR only after it was fixed, so AA-LCR is held out: its pilot shaped the floor, and its default policies had run by then, but no setting was chosen on its scores (Table~\ref{tab:log}, in the appendix). The \emph{position recipe} and the \emph{BM25 recipe} take the same four steps but rank by position or by BM25 alone, so each differs from the \recipe{} only in its ranking.

\paragraph{Oracle.} Because the needles can be found in the conversation, we can build an oracle from the \recipe. Chunks that overlap the target turn are offered first, then those overlapping the other needle turns, then the rest in the recipe's order. The information on the needle's location is only used to rank the chunks; chunk content, packing and prompt format are the recipe's. The oracle shows what the recipe's prompt achieves when ranking never misses the answer.

\paragraph{Provenance and evidence recall.} Each answer records the character ranges its prompt kept, and truncated prompts can be rebuilt exactly; Figure~\ref{fig:spans} draws these ranges for one AA-LCR prompt. MRCR is the only benchmark here that marks where its evidence is, so only there can we tell whether each prompt holds the complete target turn and all needle turns; a turn missing any of its text is incomplete. Exact match then splits into a retrieval factor and a reading factor (Table~\ref{tab:visibility}): \emph{evidence recall} at the token budget, the share of prompts that hold the complete target turn, and exact match on those prompts.

\section{Experiments}
\paragraph{Setup.} We serve Qwen3.6-35B-A3B \cite{qwen2026qwen36}, an open-weight mixture-of-experts model with a 262K-token context window, with vLLM 0.19.1 \cite{kwon2023vllm} on four NVIDIA L40S GPUs and send it one request at a time. Decoding is greedy with thinking disabled: a reasoning trace would take thousands of tokens from the same window and so shrink, and vary, the input budget the policies share, and we wanted each policy's effect on the answer the model gives, without a separate reasoning phase. AA-LCR uses its official prompt and MRCR v2 its released prompts. On LongBench-v2 a system prompt asks for a single letter, and decoding is constrained to A--D. The answer reserve is 4,096 tokens on AA-LCR and MRCR and 8 on LongBench-v2. Within a benchmark, every condition uses the same questions, prompt template, model and serving setup, and conditions are compared question by question. Unless stated otherwise, results are at each benchmark's default window (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}).

\paragraph{Conditions.} A condition is one policy at one context window on one benchmark. The policies of Table~\ref{tab:policies} run at every window: 32K, 64K and 80K on AA-LCR, whose prompts all fit at 128K; 32K, 64K, 128K and 262K on MRCR; and 32K, 64K and 128K on LongBench-v2. Random chunks runs three seeds at each benchmark's default window, and full context runs once, at 262K. The oracle runs on MRCR only, and the position recipe on AA-LCR only: on a prompt that holds one text, position keeps its head and tail, in effect middle truncation with an omission marker. Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, in the appendix, lists all 93 conditions, including the checks and ablations reported there.

\paragraph{Grading.} MRCR and LongBench-v2 are scored deterministically against their reference answers. AA-LCR is graded as its v1.1 release specifies \cite{artificialanalysis2025aalcr,artificialanalysis2026methodology}: an LLM judge, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna at medium reasoning effort, applies the published equality checker, whose system prompt sets rules for equivalent answers. To check Luna, we also graded every answer with the served model. We then labeled by hand every answer the two graders disagree on, plus a random sample of those they agree on, without seeing either grade. We estimate Luna's accuracy at 95.3\% (95\% interval [84.9, 99.1]), and the errors we found change no run's accuracy by more than two points (Table~\ref{tab:grader}, in the appendix). AA-LCR's prompts do not ask for an answer format, and with thinking disabled the model often works through the problem in its answer; the judge grades the value the answer finally commits to. Across all AA-LCR runs, 4.6\% of answers reach the 4,096-token cap, and Luna marks 136 of these 144 as incorrect. At 64K, middle truncation reaches it on 5 answers, the \recipe{} on 2 and full context on none (Table~\ref{tab:cost}, in the appendix). Leaving out the questions on which either reaches it, the recipe still leads middle truncation by 40, 25 and 14 points at 32K, 64K and 80K, with intervals that exclude zero.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Each condition runs once. We report paired differences from middle truncation at the same window, with 95\% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples drawn with a fixed seed. Questions that share a source are resampled together (the 30 document sets of AA-LCR and the 194 texts of LongBench-v2). MRCR has only four conversations, so we resample questions within each conversation, and its intervals hold for these four conversations only. Two comparisons answer the paper's central questions, which the plan posed before any run reported here, naming position as the decisive control: the \recipe{} against middle truncation, and against position, which keeps the same structure without taking the question into account. We chose their test and its correction after the results were known. We test both at every window on every benchmark, 20 comparisons in all, with a two-sided paired randomization test that swaps the two policies' outcomes in the same groups as the bootstrap (100,000 swaps), and correct the 20 $p$-values together with Holm's method (Table~\ref{tab:primary}, in the appendix). On MRCR and LongBench-v2, where position is in effect middle truncation with an omission marker, the second comparison largely repeats the first; keeping it in the family only makes the correction stricter. We call a difference significant only when its corrected $p$-value is below 0.05. Every other comparison is exploratory, and we report its interval without correction. Greedy decoding is repeatable in our setup: the two serving nodes that ran every reported run gave identical answers to identical prompts (see the appendix).
\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the policies at each benchmark's default window, and Figure~\ref{fig:policies} follows the main policies across windows; Table~\ref{tab:windows}, in the appendix, gives the numbers at every window. Differences are in points, against middle truncation at the same window unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Middle truncation costs much of the score, and query-aware truncation recovers most of it.} On AA-LCR at 64K, middle truncation scores 40\%, against 65\% for full context, and the \recipe{} scores 64\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}). On MRCR at the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the conversation that fits to 10\% on the longest, while the recipe scores the same as the oracle on every conversation, 63\% on the longest (Figure~\ref{fig:mrcr-length}, in the appendix). At this window the 133K conversation fits and 97\% of the 267K one does, so the gain comes from the two longest conversations, 60 of the 120 questions. MRCR's query also nearly repeats the needle requests: querying with the full question instead costs dense ranking 9 points (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). LongBench-v2 leaves little to recover: full context is only 5.9 points above middle truncation at 64K. That gain lies in single-document QA, long in-context learning and structured data, and both recipes gain in the first two as well; on multi-document QA, no policy beats middle truncation (Table~\ref{tab:categories}, in the appendix). The recipe's gain over middle truncation is significant at every window on AA-LCR and MRCR, and at none on LongBench-v2 (Holm-corrected randomization tests, Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Changing the truncation itself does not help: marking the cut or keeping only the start of the documents moves the score by at most 2 points (Table~\ref{tab:other}, in the appendix).

\paragraph{Ranking by the question helps, and on AA-LCR so does keeping every document.} Random chunks and position, which ignore the question, never beat middle truncation with an interval that excludes zero (Tables~\ref{tab:main}, \ref{tab:windows} and~\ref{tab:other}). At each benchmark's default window, every policy that ranks by the question does, except dense ranking on LongBench-v2 (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:other}). Since every policy at a window sends about the same number of tokens, what separates them is the text they keep, not the shorter context to which \citet{gao2026ink} trace most of the benefit of filtering. In the primary test, the \recipe{} is significantly better than position at every window on MRCR and at 32K and 64K on AA-LCR, but not at 80K or on LongBench-v2 (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). Position has none of the recipe's steps, so these margins mix ranking with the steps; on AA-LCR, the position recipe, which takes the steps but ranks by position, separates the two. The steps help even without the question: the position recipe keeps text from nearly every document and gains 21 points over middle truncation at 32K, while position leaves at least one document out for 86 of the 100 questions (Figure~\ref{fig:spans}). Fusion ranking adds 9 to 16 points to the same steps, with intervals that exclude zero at 32K and 64K, but BM25 ranking adds 1 to 11, with an interval that excludes zero only at 80K (Table~\ref{tab:windows}).

\paragraph{Neither ranking dominates.} With the recipe's steps, BM25 ranking leads fusion at 7 of the 10 windows, but only two of the differences have intervals that exclude zero, one in each direction: fusion leads on AA-LCR at 64K, by 11 points, and BM25 on MRCR at 64K, by 12, as expected of a query that nearly repeats the needle requests (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The BM25 recipe needs no embedding model.

\paragraph{On MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where it misses the target.} In Table~\ref{tab:visibility}, no prompt with an incomplete target turn gets an exact match, as expected of a task that copies the target. Where the \recipe{} keeps the target complete, it is never more than one answer behind the oracle on the same questions. Dropping the other needles changes the task, though, so the oracle is not an upper bound: at 32K, the recipe's lead over the oracle on those questions, 44 answers against 39, comes from prompts that keep fewer than four needle turns, and on the 11 that keep all four the two agree (Table~\ref{tab:needles}, in the appendix). What it misses is the target itself: at 32K its evidence recall is 48\% and it recovers 43\% of the oracle's gain over middle truncation, and both rise to 100\% at 262K. The BM25 recipe has higher evidence recall and so recovers more at every window below 262K. When the needles are guaranteed, less context helps, as input length alone degrades reasoning \cite{levy2024same,gao2026ink}: the oracle scores 75.0\% at 32K against 61.7\% at 262K (+13.3 [+4.2, +22.5]).

\paragraph{On about half the input tokens, the recipe cannot be told apart from full context.} On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2, where the model could read every prompt whole, a smaller window is also an input-token budget. At 64K, the \recipe{} differs from full context by $-$1.0 points [$-$8.7, +8.0] on AA-LCR and +0.5 [$-$4.0, +4.8] on LongBench-v2, while sending 58\% and 48\% of full context's input tokens (Tables~\ref{tab:windows} and~\ref{tab:cost}, in the appendix).


\section{Limitations}
All results come from one model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with thinking disabled; a reasoning phase might make up for missing text or compound its loss. On AA-LCR and LongBench-v2 the window is imposed on a model that reads 262K, and a model built for a 64K window may use it differently. The samples are small (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}), and MRCR's intervals hold for its four conversations only. The recipe was fixed on the MRCR and LongBench-v2 questions we report, and MRCR's query and chunk size on MRCR itself; only AA-LCR is held out, and a pilot on it motivated the floor. The position and BM25 recipes were designed after the \recipe's results were known, and the position recipe ran on AA-LCR only. We did not test tail truncation, a cross-encoder reranker or prompt compression \cite{jiang2024longllmlingua}. Adding the recipe's steps one at a time cannot show which step helps most: only the neighboring chunks on AA-LCR have an interval that excludes zero, and the same step lowers the score on LongBench-v2. On LongBench-v2, the three random seeds span 39.8 to 46.6\%, as wide as the recipe's gain over middle truncation. Without a no-context run we cannot tell how much of its score the model's priors explain, and its questions leave out the prompts over 262K, which overflow without an imposed window. MRCR is synthetic, and its query nearly repeats the needle requests, which favors lexical ranking. The oracle and evidence recall need the evidence's location, known only on MRCR, so their account of why selection falls short rests on one synthetic benchmark. AA-LCR scores depend on an LLM grader, whose accuracy we can bound only at 84.9\% or more and which graded 3 of 276 recurring answers inconsistently, and on a 4,096-token answer cap, which answers reach more often under middle truncation than under the recipe, and never under full context.

\section{Conclusion}
In line with \citet{arjmandi2026distractor}, long-context evaluations that cut out the middle of overflowing prompts measure their overflow policy as well as the model. On MRCR at the 262K window, middle truncation falls from 60\% on the conversation that fits to 10\% on the longest, and on AA-LCR at 64K it scores 25 points below full context. At the same token budget, query-aware truncation recovers most of this loss: the \recipe{} scores 64\% on AA-LCR at 64K and 63\% on the longest MRCR conversation, significantly above middle truncation at every window on both benchmarks. On LongBench-v2, where full context itself gains little over middle truncation, its gain is significant at no window. Ranking by the question recovers more than ranking that ignores it, though on AA-LCR keeping text from every document also helps, and neither BM25 nor fusion ranking dominates. At smaller windows on MRCR, the recipe falls short of the oracle only where its prompt drops part of the target.

Evaluations that must truncate should therefore report their overflow policy, how much of each prompt it keeps and, where the evidence is marked, its evidence recall, alongside their scores. Where the model could read the whole prompt, query-aware truncation also saves input tokens: at 64K, the recipe cannot be told apart from full context on AA-LCR or LongBench-v2 while sending 58\% and 48\% of full context's input tokens. Future work should test other models, enable thinking, and extend the oracle and evidence recall to benchmarks of real documents that mark where the evidence is.

\section{Appendix: Serving Nodes}
Every reported run was served by one of two nodes with the same GPUs. A third node, which first served the \recipe's runs and the BM25 runs at the windows other than each benchmark's default window, answers identical prompts differently from the other two. We reran those runs on one of the two nodes, as decided before the reruns and whatever their results, and report only the reruns. Across the 17 rerun runs, the third node's scores differ from the reruns' by up to 6 points, 1.5 on average, and change up to 20 of the 100 AA-LCR answers; the largest gap is the \recipe{} on AA-LCR at 64K, 58\% there against the 64\% we report. Single-run differences of a few points are therefore within what a change of serving node can produce. On the two nodes, greedy decoding is repeatable: the \recipe{} at each benchmark's default window builds the same prompts as the final ladder rung and repeats its answers to all 120 MRCR questions on the other node and to all 221 LongBench-v2 questions thirteen days later. On AA-LCR, the recipe with and without the document floor give the same answer to all 191 questions for which they built the same prompt.
